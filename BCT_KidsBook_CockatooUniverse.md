# The Cockatoo Who Solved the Universe
### A True Story (Mostly)
*By Michel Robert Cabrié*
*Illustrated by [Artist TBD]*

---

**Page 1**

Every morning, before the sun was properly awake, forty Gang Gang Cockatoos came to drink from Michel's pond.

They had red hats and grey coats and very serious expressions.

Michel had built the pond just for them.

*(Illustration: Dawn light, a small freshwater pond in the Australian bush. Forty cockatoos with bright red crests drinking, bathing, watching. Michel watching them from the window with a cup of tea.)*

---

**Page 2**

Michel was trying to solve a puzzle.

Not a jigsaw puzzle.
Not a crossword puzzle.

The biggest puzzle there is.

**Why does the universe work?**

*(Illustration: Michel at his desk, papers everywhere, two cats asleep on the most important ones. Equations on sticky notes. A half-eaten biscuit.)*

---

**Page 3**

Scientists had been trying to solve this puzzle for a very long time.

Very smart scientists.
With very large computers.
And very long equations.
With twenty-six numbers they had to guess.

Michel didn't have a large computer.
He had a phone.
And two cats named Nyssa and Tegan.

*(Illustration: Split panel — left: enormous particle accelerator, hundreds of scientists, huge computers. Right: Michel's small desk, phone, Nyssa sitting on the keyboard, Tegan knocking a pen off the table.)*

---

**Page 4**

One day Michel thought:

*"What if you packed spheres together as tightly as possible?"*

*(Just like oranges at the fruit shop. Or bubbles in your bath.)*

*"What shape are the gaps between them?"*

*(Illustration: Close-up of oranges stacked at a market. Then bubbles in a bath. Then — zooming in impossibly small — the same pattern at the Planck scale, glowing.)*

---

**Page 5**

He measured the gaps.

There were two kinds.

A bigger gap. He called it the **octahedral void.**
*(It had eight sides.)*

A smaller gap. He called it the **tetrahedral void.**
*(It had four sides.)*

He multiplied them together.
He divided by π.
*(π is the number that comes from circles. It starts with 3.14159...)*

*(Illustration: Simple, beautiful diagram. Two different-sized glowing gaps between spheres. An equals sign. Then a very small number: 0.0074081)*

---

**Page 6**

He got a very small number.

**0.0074081**

He stared at it.

He stared at it some more.

Nyssa walked across the keyboard.

He stared at it again.

*(Illustration: Michel staring at the screen. Nyssa walking across. The number glowing on the screen.)*

---

**Page 7**

Then he did one more small sum.

And he got:

**1/137.018**

He checked it three times.

He checked it on a different calculator.

He asked Tegan what she thought.

*(Tegan knocked another pen off the table. This meant yes.)*

*(Illustration: Michel's face — realisation dawning. Tegan on the edge of the desk, one paw pushing a pen. The pen falling. Michel looking slightly stunned.)*

---

**Page 8**

**1/137** is one of the most famous numbers in all of physics.

It's called the fine structure constant.

It decides how strong electricity is.
It decides how big atoms are.
It decides why light has colours.
It decides why chemistry works.
It decides why YOU exist.

Nobody knew where it came from.

Until the gaps between spheres.

*(Illustration: The number 1/137 in the centre of the page, radiating outward — lightning bolts, atoms, rainbows, flowers, a child's face, stars, the cockatoos.)*

---

**Page 9**

Michel kept going.

From the same three numbers — the same gaps between the same spheres — he worked out:

🔵 How heavy the proton is.
🟢 Why there are exactly three kinds of matter.
🌊 Why water bends at exactly the right angle to make life possible.
🐝 Why bees buzz at exactly 350 times per second when they shake flowers.
🦫 Why beavers build their dams the way they do.
🦘 Why wombats make cube-shaped... well. Ask your parents.

*(Illustration: Each item with a small illustration — proton, three coloured matter families, water molecule, blue-banded bee on a flower, beaver dam, wombat looking dignified.)*

---

**Page 10**

He called his model **BCT.**

*(Body-Centred Tetragonal. Which is just a fancy name for "spheres packed very neatly.")*

He wrote it all down.
210 letters long.
With 259 predictions.
And zero — **zero** — made-up numbers.

*(Illustration: A towering stack of papers. Michel standing next to it, small. The papers going up and up, past the clouds, into space.)*

---

**Page 11**

He put it all on the internet for free.

Anyone could read it.
Anyone could check it.
Anyone could find a mistake — if there was one.

Nobody found a mistake.

*(Illustration: Computers and phones all over the world, people of all ages reading, checking calculators, looking surprised and delighted.)*

---

**Page 12**

Every morning, the Gang Gang Cockatoos came back to drink from the pond.

Michel watched them and thought:

*The bees already knew.*
*The beavers already knew.*
*The wombats already knew.*
*(In their own way.)*

*The cockatoos probably knew too.*
*They just weren't telling.*

*(Illustration: The cockatoos at the pond. One of them looks directly at the reader with an extremely knowing expression.)*

---

**Page 13**

The universe is made of geometry.

Spheres packed tightly.
Two kinds of gaps.
Three numbers.
Everything else.

You can check it yourself.
Right now.
On any calculator.

*(√2 − 1) ÷ 2 × (√6 − 2) ÷ 4 ÷ π*

Go on. Try it.

*(Illustration: A child sitting cross-legged with a calculator. The calculation glowing above them. The answer: 0.0074081. Stars all around.)*

---

**Page 14**

Michel still lives in a very small town.

Population: 28.

He still has two cats.
He still has forty cockatoos.
He still has one freshwater pond.

And the universe still fits inside it.

*(Illustration: Aerial view of tiny Barrys Reef — 28 houses, trees, the pond glimmering. Zooming out to show Victoria, Australia, Earth, the solar system, the galaxy — and then zooming back in to the pond, where the cockatoos are drinking.)*

---

**THE END**

*(Last page: One cockatoo looks at the reader. Speech bubble: "We knew.")*
