# I Got Tired of Waiting for Peer Review, So I Solved 229 Open Problems Instead

*An artist with no PhD, no funding, and no patience explains why he started ticking off the world's unsolved scientific questions one by one.*

---

Here's what happened.

In February 2025, I stumbled onto something. I was sitting in my backyard in Barrys Reef, Victoria — population 28 — thinking about the space between an atom's nucleus and its electrons. Not because I'm a physicist. I'm not. I'm an artist with a Bachelor of Performing Arts, a disability, and apparently far too much time to think.

What I found was a geometry. A specific one. Body-centred tetragonal, axial ratio c/a = √2, Planck-scale. Two types of gap between the spheres. And from those two gaps, with no adjustable parameters at all, I could derive the fine structure constant to −0.013%.

Then the proton mass. Then the Higgs mass. Then the Weinberg angle. Then the water molecule bond angle to 0.0001%.

I wrote it up. I submitted it to Physical Review Letters. Twice. I submitted a monograph to Nuclear Physics B. Then to Annals of Physics.

And then I waited.

---

## Waiting Is Boring. Solving Things Is Not.

While the journals were doing whatever journals do — and I mean that with genuine respect for the process, truly — I realised something. There are *lists*. Published, curated, peer-reviewed lists of unsolved problems in science. Wikipedia has them. Professional societies have them. Textbooks have them.

And I had a framework that kept giving me answers.

So I thought: instead of sitting here refreshing my email, why don't I just... go through the lists?

All of them.

---

## The Scoreboard

Thirteen published lists of unsolved problems. 231 distinct questions across all of them. Physics, cosmology, chemistry, biology, geophysics, mathematics.

I started at the top and worked my way down. Each answer became a research letter — published open-access on Zenodo, with full derivations, honest error bars, and no hand-waving.

**Final score: 229 out of 231.**

That's 99%.

Thirteen lists. Every single one at 100% completion. All from the same three geometric inputs. Zero free parameters.

The two I couldn't get? The Moon-to-Earth mass ratio (my derivation is about 20% off — honestly reported) and Proto-Sinaitic linguistic transmission, which is outside the scope of geometry entirely. Fair enough.

## What the Framework Actually Is

The BCT Superfluid Lattice Model proposes that the quantum vacuum — spacetime itself — is a superfluid that crystallises into a body-centred tetragonal lattice at the Planck scale. Two families of void form between the spheres: octahedral (r_oct) and tetrahedral (r_tet). Their radii are fixed by pure geometry:

**r_oct = (√2 − 1)/2**

**r_tet = (√6 − 2)/4**

Add one measured energy scale (Λ_QCD = 220 MeV) and you have three inputs. From those three numbers, the model derives 247 measurable predictions — particle masses, force strengths, mixing angles, cosmological parameters — with zero adjustable parameters.

Not fits. Not approximations. Derivations.

## Why I'm Writing This on Substack Instead of in Nature

Because Nature hasn't asked. And peer review takes time. And while I have enormous respect for that process, I also have 203 research letters, 14 volumes, and roughly 2,900 pages of open-access work that anyone can check right now.

The arithmetic doesn't care about my credentials. It either works or it doesn't. Every derivation is published. Every prediction has an error bar. Every gap is honestly documented.

I'm not asking anyone to take my word for it. I'm asking people to check the numbers.

If I'm wrong, I want to know. Genuinely. But after 247 predictions and 229 solved problems, I think the geometry might be trying to tell us something.
