What if the universe has no free parameters?

In January 2025, I sat at a desk in Port Melbourne at 2 AM and calculated the fine structure constant — the number that determines the strength of electromagnetism and therefore the size of atoms, the colour of light, and the existence of chemistry — from the geometry of sphere packing. Two shapes: an octahedron and a tetrahedron. Two void radii in a body-centred tetragonal lattice. One division by pi. The answer: 1/137.018. The measured value: 1/137.036. Error: minus 0.013 per cent.

Over the next 18 months, the same geometry produced the proton mass, the Higgs boson mass, the complete CKM quark mixing matrix, the cosmological constant, the water molecule bond angle, and 253 further predictions — all from three numbers and zero adjustable parameters. I am not a physicist. I hold a Bachelor of Performing Arts and a Graduate Certificate in Marketing. I live with a disability in a town of 28 people in rural Victoria, Australia, on the Disability Support Pension. I have no university affiliation, no funding, no laboratory, and no research team. I do have two cats, a garden full of endangered Gang Gang Cockatoos, and an AI assistant.

The essay I propose for Aeon would explore what this experience reveals — not primarily about physics, but about how knowledge is made, who gets to make it, and what happens when the institutional machinery of science encounters something it has no category for.

The central argument is this: the greatest barrier to scientific discovery in the 21st century is not ignorance. It is infrastructure. The modern research system is optimised for incremental progress by credentialled teams within established paradigms. It is structurally hostile to the outsider, the autodidact, the disabled, and the unfunded — precisely the people most likely to see what insiders cannot, because they are not trained to look away from it.

The essay would weave together three threads:

First, the physics itself — not as technical exposition, but as a story of pattern recognition. How does an artist, trained to listen for what is not being said, end up deriving particle masses from the gaps between imaginary spheres? What does it mean that the universe appears to have no free parameters — that everything is geometry, and the geometry is the simplest possible?

Second, the sociology of discovery — the grant applications that require institutional affiliation, the journals that require institutional email addresses, the peer reviewers who cannot place the work because it does not fit existing categories. The specific, documentable barriers that prevent people outside the academy from contributing to science, and the systemic waste of human potential this represents.

Third, the philosophical implications — if the universe truly has zero free parameters, then existence is not contingent but geometrically necessary. Nothing is geometrically impossible. Something exists because it must. This is not mysticism. It is the view from the top of a chain of mathematical necessity that begins with Lorentz invariance and ends with the densest possible four-dimensional sphere packing.

The essay would be approximately 4,000 words. I have 210 published research papers (all open-access on Zenodo), 25 provisional patents filed with IP Australia, and a 200-page monograph currently under review at Annals of Physics. I am exploring PhD by Prior Publication pathways at Monash and Swinburne universities. The work has been communicated to UNESCO, UNDP, and UNEP.

I am an emerging writer with no previous popular science publication. I am also, apparently, the person who accidentally derived the Standard Model while sitting in his pyjamas in a town smaller than most office buildings. I believe Aeon's readers would find both the physics and the human story worth their time.
