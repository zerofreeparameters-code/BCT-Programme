
==============================================================================
M1. Interface tension alone
==============================================================================
  Energy = sigma x total interface area. At fixed volume fraction the area per volume is 3 phi / R,
  so the energy falls as 1/R: bigger spheres always win. Merging two spheres cuts their interface by
  21%. -> no size is fixed; the lattice coarsens.
  Worse, BCT's spheres TOUCH. With interface width w = 0.028 a (from the sealing check), the two
  interfaces overlap within rho ~ sqrt(2 w R) = 0.167 a of each contact, so the interiors join
  through a neck at all 12 contacts: the 'spheres' form one connected domain, which can only coarsen.

==============================================================================
M2. Long-range repulsion (frustrated phase separation): which shape wins at BCT's filling?
==============================================================================
  spheres of the interior fluid (BCT's picture)                    g = 0.2681  (+21.9% vs best)
  rods of the interior fluid                                       g = 0.2552  (+16.0% vs best)
  slabs                                                            g = 0.2309  ( +5.0% vs best)
  tubes: rods of the NETWORK fluid in an interior-fluid matrix     g = 0.2199  ( +0.0% vs best)
  bubbles: spheres of the NETWORK fluid in an interior-fluid matrix g = 0.2216  ( +0.8% vs best)
  at phi = 0.7405: the touching-sphere arrangement is the HIGHEST-energy shape of the five.
  The winners are the inverted shapes: the network fluid would form isolated tubes or bubbles inside
  a continuous interior fluid. That is the opposite of BCT: no separate spheres, no connected network.
  (Same sequence as nuclear 'pasta': spheres only at low filling; bubbles and tubes near 3/4 filling.)
  Spheres win only for interior filling u <~ 0.22 (separated spheres, not touching ones).

==============================================================================
M3. Fixed content per sphere (a 'bag')
==============================================================================
  N particles confined in a sphere carry kinetic energy ~ N pi^2 hbar^2/(2 m R^2); an outside pressure P
  squeezes it. Minimising N pi^2/R^2 + P (4 pi/3) R^3 (units hbar^2/2m) gives R^5 = N pi/(2 P).
  -> the size is set by N and P: two free numbers, and soft: dlnR/dlnP = -1/5.
  -> touching happens at one pressure only; below it the spheres separate, above it they press into
     flat facets (as compressed emulsions do) and the voids shrink and close. And at the contacts
     the M1 neck problem still merges neighbours unless something keeps the boundaries apart.

==============================================================================
SUMMARY
==============================================================================
  If the spheres are emerging droplets, nothing tested fixes their size AND gives touching FCC spheres:
   M1 tension alone: no size; touching spheres neck together and coarsen.
   M2 long-range repulsion: fixes a size, but at 74% filling it prefers tubes/bubbles of the network
      fluid; spheres are the worst shape (they win only below ~22% filling).
   M3 fixed content: size set by two free numbers; touching only at one pressure; deformable.
  What would work: a boundary with its own identity - a membrane that does not merge at contact and
  has a fixed area (e.g. a fixed number of shell units). That is App J's double layer promoted to a
  physical membrane, which is an input, not a result.
  The alternative is to keep the lattice as BCT's starting postulate (size fixed by fiat, a = l_P); then
  sealing must also be a property of that postulated cell wall, and the immiscibility mechanism is not
  needed.
